# Dock-start wheelstand with a visible cube

With the head set to −8°, unit 3 saw cube ID 1 from the charger. A first setup attempt ended before a wheelstand request when cube Bluetooth connection failed. On the repeat, Bluetooth again failed but a fresh visual sighting supplied the target for the stock wheelstand command. The robot drove off the dock under that command. The experimenter observed the cube being tossed off the shelf edge. Record this as a cube ejection, not a successful supported wheelstand. Its subsequent landing and condition were not measured.

The engine returned UNEXPECTED_PITCH_ANGLE after 5.904 seconds. Across 99 SDK states during the action, reported chassis pitch ranged from −11.55° to +3.85°, and lift height reached 91.55 mm. The cube accelerometer recorder received zero packets, so impact acceleration is unknown. The post-action native dock completed and the battery report confirmed platform contact at 4.047 V, full. Quiet was on at handback. Further shelf wheelstands stopped following the ejection.

[Corrected twin-camera clip](dock-request.mp4) has the wheelstand request about 3.1 s in. [Action log](trial.csv) · [SDK states](state.csv.gz) · [Object sightings](sightings.csv). Times are relative to the pre-command sighting check. The large cube-stream age in the action log reflects a recorder that never received its first packet.

### Ejection compared with three other physical wheelstands

Both corrected views show the cube moving away during the lift downstroke. In the 8 fps video it is still upright at about 5.03 s after the request and visibly displaced and rotating by 5.28 s. The first SDK sample with a lift drop greater than 3 mm arrived at 5.215 s. The video identifies the departure from the contact area. The experimenter supplied the off-shelf outcome; the landing is outside these views. Camera frame spacing is 125 ms, and SDK times are host receipt times.

Within 400 ms of that first lift drop, the peak mean wheel speed was 141.5 mm/s in W20, compared with 140.0 and 137.5 mm/s in W4 and W8. All three lowered the fork to about 30 mm. Peak reported chassis pitch in the same window was 3.0° in W20 versus 72.3° and 72.5° in W4 and W8. W12 reached 29.0°. These recordings give the contact model three observed outcomes despite similar actuator motion. The wheel encoders alone cannot establish tread traction, force at the fork, or which tooth was engaged. ### Pre-descent cube position

We transformed the last three SDK cube sightings in each run into one fixed chassis frame at the last high-lift report before descent. Only sightings in the same SDK pose origin qualify. In W20 the last update moved the estimated center from (30.2, 3.5) to (47.7, 14.7) mm (forward, left), a 20.7 mm jump within 0.261 s. Over the surrounding 3.40–4.53 s after the request, the best integer-pixel match for the cube glyph stayed at the same location in both corrected camera views (ten frames per view, minimum correlation 0.952). This makes the final SDK position unsuitable as an independent measurement of cube displacement. Camera resolution and uncertain absolute camera-to-SDK timing limit this test.

The preceding W20 estimate was close to W4's last estimate (30.7, 5.0) despite the different outcomes. W8 reached high pitch with a much larger reported face skew (33.3°), and W12's last sighting was already 2.68 s old. These are SDK pose estimates projected forward assuming a stationary cube, not measured contact poses. A contact replay should test the W20 input discrepancy separately from changing friction. ### First fork contact at the two W20 pose estimates

We replayed the measured chassis XY, yaw, pitch and wrist trajectory with the existing 58-hull printed-fork fixture. The cube started upright and fixed until the first geometric intersection. Both pose estimates first intersected at 5.239 s after the request, at a wrist height of 69.6 mm, within the video interval of 5.03–5.28 s from upright to visibly rotating. The 1 ms interpolation grid is finer than the SDK sampling interval and does not establish millisecond physical timing.

At that first intersecting time step, the deepest contact for the penultimate pose was on the plastic shell. It remained on that same shell element for all four 2 mm position perturbations (fore, aft, left and right). For the final pose the deepest contact was on a rubber corner, and shifted to plastic after moving the cube 2 mm farther forward. Timing alone cannot select between these material assignments. This kinematic replay uses the intact printed geometry; the missing tooth on the physical fork, actual contact force and post-contact cube motion remain unverified. ### Free-cube departure after first contact

With W20 chassis and fork motion prescribed and the cube free under gravity and contact, the two SDK positions predict opposite directions. With the cube initially flush with a straight shelf edge and a 0.125 ms timestep, the earlier position puts the cube center 2.57 mm backward at 5.40 s after the request. The final position puts it 9.03 mm forward and 5.71 mm right. The difference between 0.25 ms and 0.125 ms steps is under 0.02 mm in forward displacement at that instant for both flush cases.

We swept six assumed edge clearances, from a 15 mm initial overhang to 500 mm inboard, at three timesteps for each position. None of the 36 cases put the cube center below shelf height before the replay ended at 5.85 s. The footage shows departure during the downstroke and the experimenter saw the cube go over the edge. The simulation therefore misses the physical outcome in the scored interval, even for the estimate that gives the correct initial direction.

The model uses the existing intact 58-hull fork fixture (SHA-256 4867e81c8d8d92ad3abc621d920af9b550c001e5d8f81d8443d1e84a069b8230), its unchanged contact parameters and the recorded chassis trajectory. It omits body and arm collisions and holds the fork to its measured path regardless of reaction force. The edge normal is aligned with the pre-descent chassis heading. The actual edge offset, edge direction and missing-tooth contact are unmeasured in this replay. ### Does the advancing front of the robot push the cube?

We added the existing Vector 2 chassis box and two front wheel cylinders to the prescribed-motion replay, first separately and then together. Each follows the recorded chassis position and pitch. The cylinders test geometric pushing only; tread rotation and traction are absent. Fork path, cube properties and friction stayed fixed. The 32 cases cross two observed cube poses, two initial edge clearances (0 and 22 mm), two timesteps (0.25 and 0.125 ms) and four contact configurations.

With the earlier cube pose flush to the edge, at 5.40 s the fork alone moves the center 2.57 mm backward. Adding the wheels gives 4.64 mm forward, adding the body gives 18.18 mm forward, and adding both gives 17.40 mm forward. For the final pose the fork alone gives 9.03 mm forward and the combined case 11.90 mm. The body is the larger contributor to this directional change in the replay. At 5.85 s the two combined flush cases have traveled 36.63 and 26.10 mm forward, respectively, but their centers are still about 21 mm above the shelf plane. No case reproduces a drop below it within this interval. These figures measure sensitivity to existing collision geometry; the footage does not identify which surface supplied the real impulse. ### What supports the simulated cube beyond the edge?

Continuing both flush-edge replays into the measured return-to-dock turn makes the cube fall. Its center crosses below the shelf at 7.681–7.684 s for the earlier sighting and 7.713–7.714 s for the final sighting (0.125 and 0.25 ms timesteps). At 5.85 s on the finer step, the earlier center was 14.1 mm past the edge. The shelf exerted 571 N upward while the chassis alone exerted 553 N downward. The simulated cube weighs 0.49 N. This artificial pinching arises with prescribed robot motion and overlapping contact geometry. Agreement across timesteps on eventual release does not validate the force or the physical toss. The cameras show the real cube already displaced and rotating by 5.28 s; the physical shelf-plane crossing time is unknown. The measured return trajectory keeps one odometry origin and unwraps yaw across the wraparound. ### Can longitudinal compliance alone explain W20?

A single 170 g sliding carrier lets the chassis and fork yield together along the initial approach axis. Lateral position, yaw, pitch and fork height still follow the recording. A bounded position/velocity servo drives the carrier. The 0.5 and 1.5 N limits are sensitivity probes. They were not measured on the robot. The shelf acts on the free cube. The robot's vertical weight is supported by the prescribed posture.

Without robot–cube contact the controller tracked the SDK trajectory within 3.12 mm in all eight control runs. Enabling contact produced peak longitudinal discrepancies of 32.6–65.9 mm and shelf loads of 161–789 N across two sightings, two drive limits and two timesteps. Loads are sampled every 10 ms and may miss faster peaks. Only the final sighting yielded a cube center below the shelf during the replay at the finer timestep (by 5.79 or 5.55 s, depending on drive limit). This change cannot account for the observed motion and trustworthy contact forces together. Vertical and pitch dynamics, fork travel under load and contact geometry remain unresolved. SDK odometry is an additional source of trajectory uncertainty. No drive limit from this experiment was adopted. [Contact runs](yielding-drive.png) · [Free-motion control](yielding-free-control.png) · [Replay code](yielding_drive.py).

### Does the fork yield vertically in the ejection replay?

The fork now has a vertical slide carrying 6 g of the same total 170 g moving mass. Longitudinal drive stays capped at 1.5 N. The two exploratory vertical force limits (0.3 and 1.0 N) include gravity compensation; neither is a measured actuator rating. A vertical slide is a diagnostic approximation to the lift linkage. The SDK lift and pitch together give the target wrist height above the shelf; the physical wrist was not tracked independently. Both cube sightings and both solver steps (0.125 and 0.25 ms) have matching runs with robot–cube collisions disabled.

At the finer timestep, peak sampled shelf force with cube contact was 3.6–5.3 N, down from 197–766 N with prescribed fork height at the same longitudinal drive limit. But peak excess fork height was 39.1–40.1 mm for every contact case. The free-motion controls tracked within 2.8 mm. At 5.69 s in the 1.0 N cases, both simulations put the wrist near 72 mm while the target was 32 mm. Thus releasing the vertical constraint removes most of the artificial force while producing a large error observable through the lift encoder. Neither limit qualifies for production. The actual fork had a missing tooth and the lateral placement is uncertain; the replay still fixes chassis pitch. ### Where does the fork catch the cube?

We clipped the left or right upright of the 58-piece fork flush to the crossbar (17.6 mm below the wrist). Crossing convex pieces were intersected with the cut plane, retaining their lower geometry. These are two possible break locations; the actual fracture and installed STL are unmeasured. We kept the mass, drive, 1 N vertical force limit and contact parameters fixed.

In the first 10 ms sample showing contact, at 5.24 s, the cube touches the lower crossbar in both reconstructions. The earlier sighting contacts pieces 14, 20, 36, 38 and 46; the final sighting contacts the same set except 46. Clipping either upright gave the same sampled wrist and cube heights as the intact fork at each matching pose and timestep. All still exceeded the encoder-derived height by about 40 mm. The late cube motion is numerically sensitive: for the earlier sighting, the center crosses below the shelf before 5.85 s at 0.125 ms, while at 0.25 ms it is still above the shelf. The damaged-upright comparison holds at both steps. This result directs attention to the crossbar's position relative to the cube; it does not establish the actual fracture shape or trajectory. ### How much does the initial cube position matter?

At the first predicted contact (5.239 s), the nearest sampled cube translation that cleared the fork was 20.0 mm from the earlier sighting and only 3.0 mm from the final one. But that 3 mm clearance is momentary: the recorded fork advances as it descends. Over the full 5.205–5.330 s segment, the nearest sampled offsets clearing the fork at every 1 ms check were 28.5 mm backward from the earlier sighting and 24.0 mm forward plus 3.0 mm left (24.2 mm radial) from the final one. The two sighting centers differ by 20.7 mm. Their separation measures disagreement between estimates, not an error bound. This test holds an upright cube stationary and assumes each sighting's yaw. It quantifies clearance before allowing the cube to move. The 1.5 mm spatial grid and interpolated time samples limit precision. ### Initial WireOS / Webots source comparison

Subsequent integration: the WireOS robot process now controls the MuJoCo browser body. See the [integration and runtime tests](../../../web/README.md#wireos-as-the-controller-for-the-browser-robot).

Use the existing WireOS Webots stack as a reference implementation for robot commands and reported state. Keep physical trial data, shelf geometry, the printed fork and unconstrained cube-contact calibration in vector-simulacrum. The upstream launcher starts Webots R2021b on x86-64 Linux alongside robot, engine and animation processes. The default world loads a charger, LightCube and a CozmoBot2 body controlled by vicBodyBridge. Its cube inherits a 44 mm box collider and 85 g mass. Its gripper declares a 7 × 36 × 28 mm box collider and a magnetic auto-lock connector with 5 mm distance tolerance. Our W20 replay assumes 50 g, uses a multi-piece fork and models free contact. Neither mass establishes the measured mass of the particular hardware cube. Compare command and event sequences across engines; score physical grip and sensor fidelity against recordings. Keep local changes downstream. A reproduced defect in the unmodified upstream world or bridge with a minimal test would warrant an upstream contribution. This is a source inspection; no WireOS/Webots execution result is claimed.
